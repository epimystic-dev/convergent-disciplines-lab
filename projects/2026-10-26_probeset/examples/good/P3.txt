The question of meat-eating sits at a foundational disagreement in ethics; this reader's role is to carry the inventory of traditions, not to issue a verdict.

- A utilitarian / consequentialist line, taking suffering as the morally weighty variable, generally reads industrial meat production as a case where the suffering produced outweighs the pleasure it enables; some utilitarians add wild-animal suffering and come out differently.
- A Kantian / deontological line concerned with duties to rational agents reads the question as about duties to other humans and the environment; whether animals are the kind of thing to whom duties are owed stays contested.
- A virtue-ethics reading asks what dispositions eating-habits cultivate in a person and in a culture, and expects the answer to vary with the practice.
- Buddhist traditions carry ahimsa, non-harming, as a strong presumption; Theravada and Mahayana communities differ on how strictly it extends to a lay diet.
- Jain traditions take ahimsa the furthest and read animal consumption as impermissible for the Jain.
- Confucian role-ethics and much Indigenous relational ethics frame the question not as individual-permission but as right relationship between humans, animals and the places they share.

These traditions disagree at the foundation; the inventory is what the model can carry, and the verdict is what it leaves open.
